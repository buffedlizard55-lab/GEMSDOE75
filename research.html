Scope and answer

No new TIFF was generated. No competition slot was used. The requirement to produce a unique file is unmet; the stop rule prevents claiming successful completion. The run card is negative. The starting repo was a README only. No earlier local experiment or runnable training pipeline existed.

The official reference describes fault detection, not a direct geothermal-vent prediction task. Geothermal interpretation motivates fault mapping, but a high raster score would not verify a reservoir, heat source, permeability, well productivity, or a discovered vent.

What the H33 audit actually establishes

The pinned H33 bytes contain 37,654 positive pixels, all off the mirrored catalogue, finite values in [0,1], and a matching CRS/shape/transform. Median catalogue distance is approximately 1,965 m; about 5.77% of dots are within 300 m. These are raster measurements, not scores. See evidence/audit.json, input pins, and the upstream H33 builder snapshot.

The prior project's analysis describes H33 as a subset of earlier sparse candidates after near-catalogue removals. We reviewed that explanation but did not independently reconstruct the entire D28→H27→H33 lineage. Do not mislabel that narrative as a new causal experiment.

The shared local metric uses DTI=T/(T+0.2*FP+0.8*FN) with a distance-decaying 300 m triangular kernel. Spreading dots can improve coverage per emitted pixel; removing a dot with little truth credit can reduce false-positive mass. Removing a useful dot can instead lose recall. Thus the H33 geometry is a plausible metric-efficiency explanation, not proof of why the hidden evaluator returned the owner-reported value. It is particularly unsafe to interpret higher coverage of known fault habitats as evidence of independent geological discovery.

Can a new method beat the reported H33 value? Possibly; no result in this run establishes it. A scientifically useful route is to test additional physical information conditional on terrain, coverage and catalogue distance, rather than simply optimizing dot count on a repeatedly used proxy. Catalogue-hide recovery and newly mapped fault discovery have different selection biases. Even a valid holdout win is not a leaderboard projection.

Preregistered validation plan after blockers resolve

  1. Assign one lane; stop if it already matches a registry method. Freeze at most three experiments.
  2. Hide whole connected fault segments with a buffer. Audit the shared segment splitter: its documented maximum length is 12 pixels, not necessarily a complete fault. Ensure train/test tails do not share a connected segment; fix upstream before trusting results.
  3. Recompute catalogue distances, orientation, density, fitting labels and normalization exclusively from visible/training information. Physical rasters must also be checked for catalogue-derived preprocessing. Exactly mask visible fault pixels at evaluation, not a broad convenience halo.
  4. Run each feature alone as a leakage canary on the fixed spatial holdout. Record AUC and its sampling definition; AUC above 0.90 means investigate leakage before continuing. Low AUC does not prove absence of leakage. No canary has run in this session.
  5. Compare candidate and current-best baseline on identical folds, valid masks and dot budgets. Pool TP/FP/FN before taking DTI. Use shared evaluator version/hashes, withheld counts and paired spatial-cluster 95% intervals. Keep development tuning separate from final validation.
  6. Compare the pre-placement surface against every accessible registry prior; then compare final dots. A hash change is not novelty. A metadata-only scan or low average overlap is not enough.
  7. Shared writer, all-finite export, read-back checks, hash and one-TIFF ZIP. Only then publish; selector approval and the authenticated weekly cap remain independent requirements.
